# Residual Networks 

## Slide 1: Title Slide 

<mark>In this video lecture, we will focus on the ResNet architecture, which enables the training of very deep neural networks up to 1000 layers deep.</mark> 

## Slide 2: Introduction 

<mark>ResNets are one of the most important breakthroughs in neural network design, after the original AlexNet. The ResNet architecture enables training of very deep networks. The authors of ResNet first carried out some experiments of training networks, with additional layers. As shown in figure (a), the VGG-19 network was expanded with additional layers inserted. When attempts were made to train this deeper architecture, it was found by the authors to have a higher training error than the simpler network for a given number of training iterations. The authors concluded that deeper networks have exponentially low convergence rates, which makes them impractical to train compared to shallower implementations. As shown on the right of figure (a), the ResNet uses residual connections, which enable training of deeper networks. In the next few slides, we will discuss the concept of residual learning and residual blocks and why this approach enables training of very deep networks.</mark> 

## Slide 3: Residual Learning & Residual Block 

<mark>The basic residual block used in ResNet is shown in figure (a). It is seen to consist of a main path with convolution and activation layers and a residual path from the input that is added to the main path before a final activation layer. In training deep network without residual paths, it can be assumed the block will eventually converge to some desired mapping function H(x). With residual learning, the main path is instead assumed to converge to the mapping F(X), where the F(X) equals H(X)-X. The original mapping (HX) will thus be realised by F(X)+X, and we can see how X is added with the shortcut or feedforward path. These residual mappings are found to be easier to train than regular layers.</mark> 

## Slide 4: ResNet Connectivity 

<mark>A potential explanation as to why using residual blocks improves training is given in the paper by Veit. In this paper and as shown in the figure, an unravelled view can be obtained of the various paths through the network. This unravelled viewpoint allows us to consider ResNets as an ensemble of networks. This ensemble-like structure explains why it is found that in ResNets that a residual block can be removed and performance is not greatly affected. Many of these sub-networks in the ensemble have a short path from input to output. During training, the short paths are observed to carry the larger magnitude gradient values and, hence, these short paths determine weight updates. Thus, the conclusion is that residual connections enable deep networks by shortening effective paths and any issues with vanishing gradients is avoided. The explanation is somewhat contradicted by the original ResNet paper authors who say that issues in deep networks without residual connections is "unlikely" to be caused by vanishing gradients. The exact reason why shortcut connections in neural networks are beneficial for training is still an open topic of research. However, there is no doubt about their effectiveness in enabling training of deep networks.</mark> 

## Slide 5: ResNet Variations 

<mark>The completed ResNet is formed from the stacking of many residual blocks giving a regular repeating structure. As can be seen in table 1, after an initial 7 x 7 convolution and max pool layer, each ResNet of different depth is formed from a stack of repeating units. Figure (a) and (b) show the residual blocks used in the parts of the resnet-18 and resnet-101 where the feature maps are of size 56 x 56. In ResNet 18, 2 convolution layers are used, while in ResNet 101 bottleneck 1 x 1 convolutions are used to reduce computation.</mark> 

## Slide 6: Batch Normalisation I 

<mark>ResNet also uses the batch normalisation layers to improve training speed. Batch norm is widely adopted and used in many modern networks. It is also shown to help regularisation during training. In the original Resnet, the batch normalisation layer is inserted after convolution. As can be seen in the batch normalisation algorithm shown on the slide, the mean and variance of the input data is obtained over a number of batches during training. The data is first normalised and then two parameters, gamma and beta, are used to scale and offset the data. These two parameters are independently learned for each batch norm layer in the network. The original batch normalisation paper referred to this normalisation process as "reducing the internal covariate shift". The covariate shift is the changes, which occur at the input to each layer due to weight updates during training. By including this normalisation layer, the overall statistics of the input to each layer will tend to be more consistent during training, helping to improve convergence. As each batch normalisation layer only has to learn a scale and a shift parameter, this doesn't add greatly to the number of parameters in the model.</mark> 

## Slide 7: Batch Normalisation II 

<mark>The exact reason for why batch normalisation improves training speed and allows large learning rates is still an open question. In the paper reference [8], they argue against the covariate shift explanation for batch normalisation. Instead, they carry out a series of experiments showing the effect of training with and without batch normalisation. In figure (a), training and test accuracy is determined for networks with and without batch norm. It is observed that networks without batch norm can only train with a low learning rate and take a long time to converge compared to those with batch norm and a higher learning rate. Lower test accuracy is also achieved with low learning rates even with batch norm. The authors further show experimental results that show that batch norm prevents internal states in deep layers of the network from exploding during training. Figure (b) shows the mean and variance of layers 8, 44 and 80 of ResNet 110 without batch norm during a training step where the mean and variance of the layers explode as the gradient update is completed. The difference in scale for each of the layers must be noted, and the values of the variance in one particular channel of layer 80 is seen to reach 1e16. By including batch normalisation the activations of each channel are corrected and this prevents activations from accumulating through the network, particularly in the deeper layers, so that this type of divergent behaviour does not occur.</mark> 

## Slide 8: Squeeze Excite Network [9] 

<mark>The family of squeeze excite networks are created by modification of inception or ResNets with the addition of so-called squeeze and excite blocks. The squeeze excite block can be inserted into a ResNet residual block just between the last convolution in the main path and the shortcut addition.</mark> 

<mark>A squeeze excite block is shown in figure (a). The input is a volume of spatial dimension height by width containing a number of channels C. A global average pooling stage is applied, which reduces the spatial dimension down to 1 x 1 still with C channels. This is then applied to a cascade of fully connect layers with activations. The last activation is a sigmoid, which limits the values between 0 and 1. These final activations are used as scaling factors on each individual channel of the input. The function of this squeeze excite block is to allow interdependencies between channels to be modelled and utilised. The squeeze excite block is computationally lightweight and as mentioned can be inserted into ResNet or Inception. There is a 10% improvement in ImageNet for ResNet 50 by adding squeeze excite blocks to the network.</mark> 

## Slide 9: DenseNet I 

<mark>The final network that we will look at is called DenseNet. This may be viewed as taking the concept of ResNet but extending the amount of connectivity in the network. At the top of figure (a), we can see the DenseNet. Similarly to ResNet, we first convolve the input with convolution stage with a large kernel and then apply max pooling. The remainder of the network consists of larger dense blocks split by simple transition blocks containing 1 x 1 convolutions and average pooling. The network is terminated with global average pooling and a softmax layer. Each dense block is shown in figure (a). The blocks consist of composite functions containing convolution, batch norm and activation functions. We will detail the composite function on the next slide. It is similar to a ResNet layer except, instead of summing the shortcut path with the main path, in DenseNet the output feature maps are concatenated instead. The result using concatenation is that the number of output feature maps grows after each composite function. As can be seen, if the input has k0 channels, and each composite function adds k new features, the number of channels after each composite function is nk+k0. Finally, in figure (a), we show how the effect of including concatenation operations creates a dense connectivity. Each composite function has an input from all previous stages including the input. The benefit of this dense connectivity is similar to ResNet, we have many short paths and avoid any problems with exploding or vanishing gradients. Despite introducing much more connectivity than ResNet, an equivalent performing DenseNet may have much less parameters, up to 90% according to the DenseNet paper. DenseNet may be considered to be a less redundant representation of the data. The DenseNet architecture is narrow but deep, only a limited number of kernels are included in each dense block. As we have already mentioned, we combine the shortcut around the layers by concatenation not summation. This can lead to a large number of output feature maps despite the limited number of parameter.</mark> 

## Slide 10: DenseNet II 

<mark>On this slide, we can see the composite function used in a DenseNet. The composite function shown contains batch norm. The DenseNet with batch norm is called DenseNet-B. As can be seen in figure (a), we use a different ordering of the batch norm, convolution and activation layers, compared to the original ResNet. The paper on "Identity mappings in deep residual networks" proposes this alternative ordering as being superior to the original. As mentioned, the Denseblock uses a narrow architecture. Thus the main 3 x 3 convolution has k kernels, typically in a DenseNet k = 32, in ResNet we see 64 and 256 channels in Residual blocks. Once again, we also see the use of bottleneck layers to reduce computation.</mark>

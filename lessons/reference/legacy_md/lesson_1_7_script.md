## Slide 3: Bias Variance Trade Off 

On this slide we will discuss the bias-variance trade off from machine learning theory. Bias and variance are sources of error in the trained model or classifier. The bias is the inherent error due to the model complexity. The Variance is the amount the classification result changes with different training data. These error sources are linked, with less complex models tending to have high bias and low variance and more complex classifier having low bias and high variance. In the upper plot in Figure (a), the classification error rate vs the model complexity is plotted. We see that the total error is a U shaped curve with high error for both low complexity and high complexity models, with the minimum corresponding to some optimal complexity classifier. In this same plot we can see the bias at a maximum for low complexity classifiers, it decreases to a minimum for high complexity classifiers. We can also see the variance is at a minimum, for low complexity classifiers and increases to a maximum for high complexity classifiers. Low complexity classifiers will have a high bias as they may be based on simple models (such as a linear classifier) and therefore can only produce simple decision boundaries that may not fit well to the data, we call this behaviour underfitting. An example of underfitting is shown in the plot on the bottom left of figure(a). We can see that with a linear classifier, some of the points from both classes appear on either side of the decision boundary. In contrast high complexity models may tend to have a large variance. A high complexity model when trained on the data may tend to overfit to the data, thus the decision boundary may be a very complex shape. This behaviour is shown in the plot on the bottom right of figure(a). The variance of the complex classifier is said to be high when the decision boundary will change significantly if different training data is used. The result of choosing an optimum complexity classifier is shown in the bottom centre plot. In this case we see that a curve is chosen as the decision boundary. The model in this case follows the general trend of the data and does not try to fit to outlier or spurious points. In summary we say that if the classifier model is too simple, we get underfitting and we have high bias and low variance, conversely if the classifier model is too complex we get overfitting with low bias but high variance. 

## Slide 4: Model Training 

Typically we can detect if a model is displaying underfitting or overfitting  during the training process, by inspection of the error achieved by the training set and validation set. The figure on this slide shows 3 cases, we will go through these from the top down. In the underfitting case the error for both the training and validation data remain high. This is to be expected due to high bias, the decision boundary does not fit the data very well and we won’t get a low classification error no matter how much training we perform. In the overfitting case we see a divergence between the training and validation data. The error on the training data continues to decrease with more training while the validation set error initially decreases then starts to increase. This behaviour is explained by overfitting of the classifier model to the training data during training. As the model overfits to the training data, it gets less able to make good predictions on the validation data. Detecting over fitting is the reason why we should always use validation data during training as just relying on training error can mislead us. Finally for the case of an optimal model, we should see both the training and validation data error reduce simultaneously to a low error value. We can mitigate against underfitting behaviour by increasing model complexity. Overfitting can be mitigated by using large dataset sizes, decreasing the model complexity and regularising the paramerters. 

## Slide 5: Generalisation 

An important machine learning concept to also discuss is generalisation. Generalisation is the ability of the model to perform well on unseen data. This is the goal of our training process, as we have already seen, it is of no value if the model just overfits to the training data as it will perform poorly on both the validation and unseen test data. It is imperative that test data is not used during training or misleading performance results will be obtained. Good generalisation requires that the model is not underfitting or overfitting. Neural networks can very easily overfit to training data and loose their abililty to generalise. We will shortly discuss how we can avoid neural networks from overfitting. 

## Slide 6: The Curse of Dimensionality 

The curse of dimensionality is another classical machine learning concept that also has implications for neural networks. When applying images to a classifier model we can think of each pixel as being a dimension of the data. Therefore, even a small image of size 256 x 256 pixels will have 65536 dimensions. In classical machine learning attempts are made to convert images to a different representation to try and reduce the dimensionality. Figure (a) shows an example of a binary classification problem where there is only one dimension in the feature vector. As can be seen in the figure we cannot easily discriminate between the classes, therefore we will need to use more dimensions in the data. As shown in figure (b), if we add a second dimension to the data we may be able to discriminate better between the classes, but we can also see that as the number of data points has remained the same, the distance between the points has generally increased. This increase in distance between the points is called sparsity, it will only increase as we include more data dimensions. With sparse training data we are likely to observe overfitting when training the classifier. If we try to offset the overfitting by adding more training data to our dataset, we find that the required amount of data increases exponentially with each dimension just to maintain the generalisation of the classifier. This exponential increase in the requirement of training data to maintain classifier generalisation is known as the curse of dimensionality. For the case of neural network we always use raw images at the input to the networks, which suggests the curse of dimensionality does apply. However due to the fact that we use convolution layers at the input which only operate on the image locally and we learn to extract features from the data before classifying in the final layers, neural 

networks are less susceptible to the curse of dimensionality than other classical machine learning algorithms. 

## Slide 7: Neural Network Overfitting 

<mark>One key feature of neural networks is that they are over parameterised. They contain a very large number of parameters and therefore without any form of regularisation will tend to form a complex model of the data and strongly overfit the training set. There are many solutions to avoid or mitigate against overfitting in neural networks, these help the network learn a less complex model that will generalise to the test data.  A simple strategy is to use early stopping, where we stop training once validation error is observed to increase as shown in figure (a). We can also employ standard regularisation techniques to reduce overfitting. This generally involves adding a penalty to the loss function to prevent large weights dominating the network. Another very common approach to preventing overfitting is dropout, as shown in figure (b). During each training update, certain neurons are randomly ignored. This prevents the network from overreliance on any one particular neuron(s) and makes the network model more robust and general. As we will see in a later lesson on neural networks for image classification, batch normalisation has become a key layer to help improve training time by allowing training with larger learning rates. Batch normalisation also has a regularising effect that helps with over fitting. A final, very obvious solution to overfitting is to use more training data. If during training, the neural network consistently sees new examples, it will prevent the network from learning the dataset through repeated exposure to the same training examples. Data augmentation can be used to expand the number of training images, for example by scaling and shifting or cropping the images to make more examples from the original dataset. We will consider some of these regularisation techniques in more detail over the next few slides.</mark> 

## Slide 8: L1 & L2 Regularisation 

<mark>L1 and L2 regularisation are widely used across various machine learning models. They operate by penalising large values of parameters in the loss function, this forces the model to use more equally sized parameters and also achieve better generalisation. L1 and L2 regularisation can also be applied to neural networks, the parameter values from convolution and dense layers can be individually penalised. The L1 regularisation term penalises the absolute values of the weights, while the L2 regularisation term is proportional to the square of the weights. Within the tensorflow keras API, these regularisation may be applied to the kernel, bias or activation, as shown in the code snippet the regularisation is added as an argument in the layer.</mark> 

## Slide 9: Dropout 

The dropout technique involves the random removal of nodes of the neural network during training. At any training step the nodes may be removed with a probability between 0 and 1. This produces a more sparse network at each training interval. Overall the use of dropout forces the network to not rely on specific paths or neurons, and therefore provides similar regularisation to the L2 regulariser by reducing the weight values. In the tensorflow/keras API, dropout is applied an independent layer. It has the effect of randomly removing connections to the preceding layer with a certain probability. Using too large a dropout rate can slow down convergence or cause underfitting. 

## Slide 10: Spatial Dropout 

Dropout was originally designed for use with fully connected neural networks, where removing connections to a neuron for a training interval has a regularising effect on the network. In the case of convolutional networks, as shown in the upper figure, the convolution kernel is applied across a region of the preceeding feature map. If dropout is applied various pixels at random are removed from the region of the feature map before multiplication with the kernel.  If the pixels in the region are highly correlated, which is likely in a CNN, then the dropout may not greatly affect the result of 

convolution between the feature map and the kernel. In spatial dropout shown in the lower figure, instead of just removing single pixels, entire feature maps are dropped out. This will have a much greater effect on specific kernel channels and prevent them from learning from specific correlated features during the training interval. 

## Slide 11: Data augmentation 

The final approach to reducing overfitting is data augmentation. Ideally we could prevent overfitting in the neural network by training it for longer with new training data, rather than repeating the same data. Unfortunately labelled datasets are finite and it is expensive and time consuming to produce new labelled training data. Data augmentation involves modifying existing data, so that variations of the images can be used as new training examples. The simplest approach to data augmentation is to apply affine transforms, that is operations such as rotations, translations, scaling and shear transforms to the data. There are also more complex augmentations that involve removal of segments of the image and combining parts of images. However the basic affine transformations will work well to prevent overfitting. The tensorflow/keras API has implemented the transforms as layers that can be included at the input to any model. The code snippet shown, implements random flip in the horizontal direction, random zoom and random translation. We don’t need to add too much augmentation as otherwise underfitting will occur, we often need to experiment to find the best level of augmentation along with use of other regularisers for a given task.

## Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to:

- explain why **PCA can reduce** classification performance and how **LDA** differs;
- compute the **scalar projection** of a feature vector onto a line and the **transformed means**;
- explain why the separation of the means alone is not a sufficient criterion, and define **scatter**;
- write down the LDA **objective function** and its solution in terms of the **within-** and **between-class scatter**.

## PCA, LDA and discrimination

In the last lesson the principal components pointed along the direction of maximum variance of the data, which let us reduce its dimensionality, and the reverse transform recovered the original data with losses that depend on how many eigenvectors were used. PCA is therefore optimised for **transformation and reconstruction**.

While reducing dimensionality generally helps classification, a disadvantage of PCA is that it takes **no account of the class labels**. In some problems the PCA transform makes classification **worse**: when the data is projected onto the principal component, the classes can become impossible to separate.

**Linear discriminant analysis** is an alternative that explicitly includes the classes of the data when transforming it. It finds a line that preserves a direction useful for classification, and that line need not lie anywhere near the principal components of the data.

{{fig pca_vs_lda}}

## Transformed data means

LDA uses statistical properties of the data to determine the line that is optimal for classification. Let the data matrix be $X$, each column a two-dimensional feature vector $x_i$, with $n_1$ vectors from class $c_1$ and $n_2$ from class $c_2$, and let $v$ be a candidate line to transform the data onto. The **scalar projection** of a feature vector onto the line is the dot product

$$y_i = v \cdot x_i = v^{T} x_i. \tag{1}$$

The transformed mean of a class is the average of the projections of its feature vectors. Taking $v^{T}$ outside the sum,

$$\hat\mu_1 = \frac{1}{n_1} \sum_{i \in c_1} v^{T} x_i = v^{T} \mu_1, \tag{2}$$

so the transformed mean is simply $v^{T}$ times the mean of the untransformed feature vectors of that class. The hat denotes a transformed quantity.

{{fig projected_means}}

## Which line?

Intuitively we might choose the line that gives the **maximum separation between the transformed means**. That is not enough. Consider two candidate lines, one horizontal and one vertical. The horizontal line gives the wider separation of the transformed means, but one of the classes has a wide variance in the horizontal direction, and after transformation the data from the two classes overlaps and is no longer separable. The vertical line separates the means less but keeps the classes apart.

The **scatter**, another word for variance, must therefore appear in our criterion.

{{fig which_line}}

### Scatter

In LDA we use scatter rather than variance: the scatter of a distribution is the same as its variance but **without** the normalisation factor of $1/(n-1)$. Defining it in terms of the projections $y_i$, and working in one dimension after the transformation, the transformed scatter of a class is

$$\hat S_1 = \sum_{i \in c_1} (y_i - \hat\mu_1)^2, \tag{3}$$

computed separately for each class.

## The LDA objective function

With the transformed means and scatters we can write an objective function that depends on the choice of line $v$:

$$J(v) = \frac{(\hat\mu_1 - \hat\mu_2)^2}{\hat S_1 + \hat S_2}. \tag{4}$$

Maximising $J$ maximises the numerator, the separation between the transformed means, and minimises the denominator, the sum of the transformed scatters. In other words we look for the line on which the class means are far apart **and** each class is tightly clustered. A line that achieves this greatly helps the classifier.

{{fig objective_function}}

## Solving for the line

Equation (4) is written in terms of transformed quantities and does not contain $v$ on the right-hand side, so we rewrite it using the means and scatter of the data **before** transformation. For two-dimensional data the scatter of a class is computed like a covariance but without the normalisation, as a sum of outer products of the mean-adjusted feature vectors, giving a 2 × 2 matrix. The **within-class scatter** is the sum of the individual class scatters:

$$S_W = S_1 + S_2, \qquad S_j = \sum_{i \in c_j} (x_i - \mu_j)(x_i - \mu_j)^{T}. \tag{5}$$

Substituting $y_i = v^{T} x_i$ and $\hat\mu_j = v^{T}\mu_j$ into equation (3) and rearranging gives $\hat S_j = v^{T} S_j v$, so the sum of the transformed scatters is the scalar

$$\hat S_1 + \hat S_2 = v^{T} S_W v. \tag{6}$$

The **between-class scatter** measures the separation of the class means before transformation. It is computed from the mean vectors only, as the outer product of their difference, and is again a matrix:

$$S_B = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^{T}, \qquad (\hat\mu_1 - \hat\mu_2)^2 = v^{T} S_B v. \tag{7}$$

Updating the objective function with these quantities gives an expression in known matrices,

$$J(v) = \frac{v^{T} S_B v}{v^{T} S_W v}. \tag{8}$$

The derivative of $J(v)$ is zero at its maximum. Setting it to zero gives a **generalised eigenvalue problem**; if the inverse of $S_W$ exists, which is true for many problems, multiplying across by $S_W^{-1}$ puts it in the form of a standard eigenvalue problem whose solution is

$$v^{*} = S_W^{-1} (\mu_1 - \mu_2). \tag{9}$$

This is easy to evaluate whenever $S_W$ can be inverted, which holds when the number of feature vectors is comfortably larger than the number of dimensions. For more complex tasks, image data in particular, $S_W$ may be **singular** and no inverse exists. The next lesson shows how the facial-recognition task avoids that problem.

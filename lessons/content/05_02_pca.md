## Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to:

- explain the **curse of dimensionality** and why images need dimensionality reduction;
- interpret a **sample covariance matrix** and the **eigenvectors** that are its principal components;
- state the steps of the **PCA algorithm**;
- use PCA to **decorrelate**, **reduce the dimension of** and **compress** data.

## Representations and supervised learning

The machine-learning lesson introduced the components of supervised learning: the dataset, the representation and the classifier. Finding a good representation is the key to training a successful classifier. A good representation removes **redundant** information by reducing the dimensionality of the feature vector, which cuts the computation in the classifier and the amount of training data needed, and it makes the feature vectors more **discriminative**. Principal component analysis is our first example.

## The curse of dimensionality

How many features does a feature vector need? Too few and the classifier cannot discriminate between the classes. Adding a second feature may separate the classes better, but with the number of data points unchanged the distance between them grows. This growth is called **sparsity**, and it increases with every feature added. Sparse training data leads to **overfitting**. Adding training data to compensate does not help much either: the amount required grows **exponentially** with each dimension merely to maintain the same generalisation, which is the curse of dimensionality. Computation also rises, both from the extra features and from the extra samples.

For a fixed number of training points there is an optimal number of features that gives the lowest classification error; increasing the number far beyond it makes performance worse. This matters acutely for images, since even a small 256 × 256 image has 65,536 features when the raw pixels are used directly. Reducing the dimensionality of image data is therefore essential.

## Principal component analysis

PCA is defined as an **orthogonal linear transformation** that maps data to a new coordinate system such that the retained sample variance is maximised. A distribution described in the coordinates $d_1, d_2$ is transformed to new axes $u_1, u_2$, chosen so that the variance of the data along $u_1$ is as large as possible; $u_1$ is then a **principal component** of the distribution. An equivalent definition is that the L2 distance from the observed points to the new axis is minimised. As we will see, the principal components of any distribution are the **eigenvectors of its sample covariance matrix**. The main benefit is a lower-dimensional, compressed representation of the data.

### The sample covariance matrix

For two-dimensional data the covariance matrix has four elements. The diagonal entries are the variances of the data in the $d_1$ and $d_2$ directions; the off-diagonal entries are the covariance of $d_1$ with $d_2$, computed by subtracting the mean from each dimension and summing the products of the paired samples:

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{var}(d_1) & \operatorname{cov}(d_1, d_2) \\ \operatorname{cov}(d_1, d_2) & \operatorname{var}(d_2) \end{pmatrix}. \tag{1}$$

The values describe the shape of the distribution. Positive covariance means that as $d_1$ increases $d_2$ tends to increase with it; negative covariance means it tends to decrease; and zero covariance means the two variables are **independent**.

{{fig covariance_shapes}}

### Eigenvectors

The eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix give the directions of **maximum variance** of the data. In two dimensions they form two orthogonal vectors aligned with the spread of the distribution, and they define a new coordinate system in which the data has **zero covariance**. They are the principal components, and they satisfy

$$\Sigma\, u = \lambda\, u, \tag{2}$$

where the eigenvalue λ associated with each eigenvector is the variance of the data along it.

{{fig eigenvectors_2d}}

## The PCA algorithm

For a distribution $X$ of $n$ sample vectors of dimension $d$:

1. arrange the vectors into a $d \times n$ matrix $X$;
2. form the mean of the samples in each dimension and **subtract** it from every vector;
3. form the sample covariance matrix $\Sigma = X X^{T}$;
4. obtain the eigenvalues and eigenvectors by **eigenvalue decomposition** of $\Sigma$, or equivalently by **singular value decomposition** of $X$;
5. order the eigenvalues and retain the $k$ largest and their eigenvectors. These are the principal components.

{{fig pca_algorithm}}

### Transformation and dimension reduction

The principal components have two important uses. Multiplying the original distribution by the eigenvectors **transforms** the data to a coordinate system in which it is no longer covariant. And multiplying by only the $k$ eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues **reduces the dimension**: a small eigenvalue means little variation along that eigenvector, so discarding it loses little information. Multiplying two-dimensional data by a single eigenvector gives a compressed one-dimensional representation.

{{fig pca_transform}}

## Image compression with PCA

PCA can compress an image. The image is divided into small patches, for example 16 × 16 pixels; each patch is flattened into a vector of length 256 and the set of patches is the input to PCA. The eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix are found, and the $k$ with the largest eigenvalues are used to transform every patch. Reshaped back into patches, the eigenvectors can be displayed as images. The compressed image is reconstructed by multiplying the retained coefficients back by the eigenvectors; there is visible loss of detail, but the image remains recognisable from a small fraction of the original data.

{{fig pca_compression}}

## PCA in Python

In Python PCA is performed with **scikit-learn**. The data is supplied as a matrix of shape (number of samples, number of features). `decomposition.PCA` creates the object, and the number of eigenvectors to return is set with `n_components`; `fit` performs the decomposition on the training data. The `transform` and `inverse_transform` methods then produce the compressed and reconstructed versions of the data.
